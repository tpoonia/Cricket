# Make a solver check every LLM pick

You can build this paper in nine days, but only if you keep the dataset almost as it is and put your effort into checking the LLM. The data you already have (Cricsheet ball-by-ball, a leakage-free ETL, proxy candidate pools, inferred roles, the E3 MILP and a validated T20I simulator) covers nearly everything you need. The one must-add is a player-type join (pace or spin, left- or right-handed batter), because Cricsheet does not record bowling type, batting hand, captain or wicketkeeper ([Cricsheet JSON format](https://cricsheet.org/format/json)). Pitch, weather and fitness data are weaker than they look. No public dataset of pre-match pitch reports exists. The ICC pitch rating is given after the match, so using it would leak the result ([ESPNcricinfo](https://www.espncricinfo.com/story/is-the-icc-s-pitch-rating-system-fit-for-purpose-1365110)). Physics research found no link between humidity and swing ([ICC news](https://www.icc-cricket.com/news/long-held-cricket-swing-theory-does-not-hold-water)). No public injury or fitness-test data exists for international players. So use one abstract concept, "venue conditions", built from earlier matches at the ground and tested as an ablation. Leave weather and fitness out, and say why. The paper's real contribution is evaluation. Close prior work shows LLMs often return answers that break the rules: on ConstraintBench no frontier model passed 65% feasibility ([ConstraintBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.22465v2)), and the only LLM cricket-selection paper tested one match and checked neither rules nor reasons ([FanCric](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.01307)). Your paper can be the first to measure, over many held-out T20Is, how often an LLM's XI breaks cricket's rules, how much a solver repair costs in simulated win probability, and whether the LLM's stated reasons are true.

## The research gap is valid teams and true reasons, not better prediction

Your TA asked you to "scope the gap before experiments". The gap is clear. Operations research has studied cricket team selection since at least 2011. Sharp and colleagues used integer programming for a Twenty20 XI ([IFORS abstract](https://iaorifors.com/paper/72432)). Ahmed, Deb and Jindal used multi-objective evolutionary search under keeper, captain, budget and four-overseas constraints ([CORE PDF](https://core.ac.uk/download/291575216.pdf)). Bhattacharjee and Saikia used binary integer programming for a "balanced" XI ([RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:spr:opsear:v:53:y:2016:i:2:d:10.1007_s12597-015-0228-3)). None of these papers uses an LLM, and none explains its choices in natural language. The single LLM paper, FanCric, built Dream11 fantasy teams with a multi-agent GPT-4o-mini system. It was evaluated on **one IPL match** and reported no constraint-violation rate and no explanation check ([FanCric](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.01307)).

General LLM-optimisation work shows why checking matters. On ConstraintBench, models answered optimisation problems directly and Gurobi verified the answers. **Feasibility was the main bottleneck: the best model reached 65.0% feasibility and no model went above 30.5% joint feasible-and-optimal** ([ConstraintBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.22465v2)). On COMPASS, GPT-5 met all hard constraints in 86.9% of travel tasks but was near-optimal in only 50.6%. The authors also saw agents give "generic justifications that hide errors" ([COMPASS](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.07043v1)). The neuro-symbolic answer is to let the LLM propose and a sound checker verify. LLM-Modulo says the soundness of such a system "is inherited from the soundness of the correctness (hard) critics" ([LLM-Modulo](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01817v3)). "Formalize, Don't Optimize" found that solver-backed pipelines were more correct than letting the LLM search by itself ([arXiv 2605.12421](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.12421)). This framing fits a NeSy-FM workshop well.

Your three selectors map directly onto this literature. **A** is the LLM alone, which is the ConstraintBench setting. **B** is the LLM followed by a solver that checks and repairs, which is the LLM-Modulo setting. **C** is the MILP alone, the classic OR baseline. Write four research questions before you run anything. RQ1 asks how often A's XI breaks hard cricket rules, and which rules. RQ2 asks how much simulated win probability B loses or gains compared with C. RQ3 asks whether the LLM's reasons are true when checked against the data the LLM was given. RQ4 asks how much of A's behaviour comes from remembering real players and real line-ups rather than from the table. Every experiment below answers one of these four. Anything that answers none of them, such as extra weather features, does not belong in the paper.

## You already hold most of the data; add only player types

### What Cricsheet gives you and what it lacks

Each Cricsheet match file contains teams, the players named for each team, registry IDs, dates, venue, city, toss, event and outcome. Every delivery lists batter, bowler and non-striker ([Cricsheet JSON format](https://cricsheet.org/format/json)). From these fields you can compute every split you need (overall, recent form, against an opponent, at a venue) using only matches before the target date. That is exactly what your leakage-free ETL already does. Cricsheet does **not** record captain, wicketkeeper, bowling type or batting hand ([Cricsheet JSON format](https://cricsheet.org/format/json)). Your keeper inference from past stumpings is the right workaround. One trap: concussion substitutes and other replacements also appear in `players`, recorded per delivery under `replacements` ([Cricsheet JSON format](https://cricsheet.org/format/json)). Check that your "actual XI" label drops players who only came on as a `match` replacement.

The Cricsheet People Register maps Cricsheet IDs to ESPNcricinfo and eleven other sites. ESPNcricinfo has the most IDs (18,835), and the register is licensed under ODC-By ([Cricsheet Register](https://cricsheet.org/register)). In October 2026 Cricsheet added a Venue Register with **591 venues and stable IDs**, which fixes the problem of venue names changing over the years. It has no latitude or longitude ([Cricsheet Register](https://cricsheet.org/register)). The newest Cricsheet blog post (7 Oct 2026) also announces "a License for match data" ([Cricsheet home](https://www.cricsheet.org/)). Read cricsheet.org/licensing before you submit and cite it in your data statement. The research could not open that page, so the exact licence text is not confirmed.

### The one join you must add: pace or spin, and batting hand

The best free source for player types is the `player_meta` table in the cricketdata R package. It has **16,101 players found on both Cricsheet and ESPNcricinfo, current to 24 March 2025** ([player_meta reference](https://pkg.robjhyndman.com/cricketdata/reference/player_meta.html)). Its column names were not confirmed, so run `names(player_meta)` first. Turn each bowling-style string into pace or spin with a simple rule: "fast", "medium" and "seam" mean pace, while "spin", "break", "orthodox" and "googly" mean spin. Players who debuted after March 2025 will be missing, and your held-out matches are recent, so expect gaps. Fill them by hand from the Wikipedia World Cup squad pages. Those pages list batting style, bowling style and "(wk)"/"(c)" tags, for example "Rohit Sharma (c) | Right-handed | Right-arm off spin" ([Wikipedia 2024 squads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Men%27s_T20_World_Cup_squads); [2026 squads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Men%27s_T20_World_Cup_squads)). Wikipedia is CC BY-SA, so release your code and IDs, not a copied table. This one join powers three things: the pace/spin convention check, the venue-conditions proxy and the checking of reasons such as "we need a second spinner".

### Squads: keep your proxy pools and measure how good they are

No free, machine-readable archive of announced T20I squads exists. Wikipedia has squads only for ICC tournaments, and CricketArchive is a paid site ([Wikipedia 2024 squads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_Men%27s_T20_World_Cup_squads); [CricketArchive](https://cricketarchive.com/Archive/Events/12/ICC_World_Twenty20_2010/Squads.html)). Your proxy pool (players from the team's previous 10 matches) uses only earlier information, so it does not leak. That puts it ahead of the obvious alternative of "everyone who played in this series". Keep the proxy pools and add one honest number to the paper: **pool coverage**, the share of the actual XI's players who were in your pool. Debutants cannot be in a pool built from past matches, so coverage below 100% is expected and should be reported. If time allows, check one tournament against the real Wikipedia squads.

### Which context data to include, test, or leave out

The table below gives the decision for each kind of context. The rule follows your TA's advice: a feature gets in only if it changes a selection decision and you can measure it before the match without leakage.

| Context | Source | Decision | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Overall and recent form (point-in-time) | Your ETL | **Include (main)** | Core of every selector's criteria |
| Role: keeper, bowling option, pace/spin, batting hand | Your role inference + `player_meta` | **Include (main)** | Needed for the rules and for checking reasons |
| Venue conditions: venue spin share of overs, venue first-innings average, both shrunk to the host-country mean | Cricsheet, earlier matches only | **Include, plus an ablation without it** | The one context feature with a clear decision link (spinner vs seamer) |
| Record against this opponent | Cricsheet | **Leave out** (optional ablation) | T20I samples are often under 5 innings, so mostly noise |
| ICC pitch rating | ICC xlsx files | **Leave out** | Given after the match (leakage); rarely adverse |
| Weather (humidity, cloud, dew) | Open-Meteo | **Leave out**, future work | Weak evidence; no start time in Cricsheet; needs geocoding |
| Fitness tests, injuries | None public | **Leave out** | Not available; the pool already handles availability |
| Match workload (balls bowled in the last 7/28 days) | Cricsheet | **Leave out**, mention as future work | Your data is internationals only, so franchise load is missing |

### LLM, solver and compute on a small budget

A cheap paid model is the safest choice for nine days. For about 1,800 calls of roughly 6,000 input and 1,200 output tokens each, the notes estimate **about $1.94 for Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite, $2.16 for Claude Haiku 5.5 and $1.40 for GPT-5-nano** at standard prices, and about half that with batch APIs ([Gemini pricing](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/pricing); [Claude pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing); [morphllm OpenAI pricing](https://morphllm.com/openai-api-pricing)). Turn "thinking" off or set it low, because reasoning tokens are billed as output. Gemini's free tier uses your content "to improve our products" ([Gemini pricing](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/pricing)). That is acceptable for public cricket data, but you must disclose it. Groq's free tier caps you at about 200k tokens a day, which would take weeks ([benchlm Groq](https://benchlm.ai/md/free-tier/groq.md)). A local Qwen3-8B (Q4) on a 16 GB MacBook Air runs at about **14 tokens per second** ([willitrunai](https://willitrunai.com/es/can-run/qwen-3-8b-on-m2-air-16gb)). That works out to roughly 2 minutes per call, which is fine for an overnight run on about 30 matches but not for the full set. Use Ollama's JSON-schema `format` option for structured output ([Ollama docs](https://docs.ollama.com/capabilities/structured-outputs)). Reviewers like to see one open-weight model, so run it on a subset. For the solver, note that **PuLP 4.0 needs Python 3.12 or newer and no longer bundles CBC** (install `pulp[cbc]`). HiGHS and OR-Tools CP-SAT are free alternatives ([PuLP GitHub](https://github.com/coin-or/PuLP)). Solve time does not matter: choosing 11 from 18 has only 31,824 possible subsets.

## Captain and coach pick eleven from a squad, before the toss

Every major board uses two stages. A national selection panel names a squad. Team management then picks the playing XI from that squad. In England the Test captain had the final say on XIs as of March 2026 ([ESPNcricinfo, Mar 2026](https://www.espn.in/cricket/story/_/id/48304517/selecting-selector-proves-tricky-ecb-find-options-thin-ground)). In Australia the head coach, Andrew McDonald, is a voting member of the panel ([cricket.com.au](https://www.cricket.com.au/news/4213686/australia-squad-world-test-championship-champions-trophy-odis-sri-lanka-caribbean-andrew-mcdonald-sam-konstas-cameron-green)). In India a five-member committee picks squads; since 19 October 2026 it has been chaired by Pragyan Ojha ([Khaleej Times](https://www.khaleejtimes.com/cricket/ojha-becomes-indias-new-chairman-of-selectors-as-powar-joins-panel)). Coach Gautam Gambhir shapes the XI and the batting order ([ESPNcricinfo, Jan 2025](https://www.espn.com/cricket/story/_/id/43626416/left-right-combination-reason-dhruv-jurel-batting-no-8-says-ryan-ten-doeschate)). Formally, the captain nominates the 11 players on the ICC team sheet, and the match referee checks the list **immediately before the toss** ([ICC T20 WC 2026 Playing Conditions, cl. 1.2.1](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/yyt7m8uh9c1uehrbvuwb.pdf)). This matters for your design. In international cricket the XI cannot depend on the toss, unlike the IPL, where teams name the XI after the toss ([Scroll.in](https://scroll.in/field/1046219/impact-player-at-ipl-2023-all-you-need-to-know-about-the-new-rule-in-indian-premier-league)). Your decision unit is therefore "management chooses 11 from a pool, before the toss", and the LLM prompt must not mention the toss.

The real rules are few. A team has 11 players ([ICC cl. 1.1](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/yyt7m8uh9c1uehrbvuwb.pdf)). A bowler may bowl **at most 4 overs** in a 20-over innings and may not bowl two overs in a row ([ICC cl. 13.9.1, 17.6](https://images.icc-cricket.com/image/upload/prd/yyt7m8uh9c1uehrbvuwb.pdf)). Since 20 ÷ 4 = 5, every XI needs **at least five players able to bowl four overs**. Teams also need a wicketkeeper. Since July 2025 teams must also name five concussion reserves, one each of batter, wicketkeeper, all-rounder, spinner and pace bowler ([Cricket Pakistan](https://cricketpakistan.com.pk/en/news/detail/icc-revamps-t20i-rules-with-key-changes-to-powerplays-concussion-repla); [Wisden](https://www.wisden.com/cricket-news/icc-modify-concussion-sub-rules-in-wake-of-india-like-for-like-controversy)). That gives you an official role list (keeper, batter, all-rounder, spinner, pace bowler) to use as your label set.

Everything else in a "balanced XI" is convention. Wisden lists five workable India T20I shapes, from "5 batters / 4 all-rounders / 2 quicks" to "the England way" of 6 batters, 4 all-rounders and 1 specialist quick ([Wisden](https://wisden.com/stories/five-ways-india-can-balance-their-t20i-side)). ESPNcricinfo says countries "typically field five specialist bowlers, with a sixth or seventh bowler from the batting unit providing a few overs" ([ESPNcricinfo, Feb 2026](https://www.espncricinfo.com/page2/content/story/1522550.html)). Some conventions are weaker than teams think. India's coaches treat left-right batting pairs as a deliberate plan ([ESPNcricinfo, Jan 2025](https://www.espn.com/cricket/story/_/id/43626416/left-right-combination-reason-dhruv-jurel-batting-no-8-says-ryan-ten-doeschate)), yet an ODI analysis found right-left pairs gain only about 3 strike-rate points against spin. The author concluded the advantage is "nowhere near as strong as teams believe" ([ESPNcricinfo, Kimber 2019](https://www.espn.co.uk/cricket/story/_/id/27102343/do-right-left-pairs-crease-work-complicated)).

Selectors name both measurable and judgement criteria. England's national selector Luke Wright cited technique, play against the short ball, character ("lots of talented players get to the international level and crumble") and record against an opponent. He said stats are necessary but limited: "You can't ignore stats but you can only tell so much from them." He also admitted that some picks were a "punt" ([Sky Sports](https://www.skysports.com/cricket/news/12040/13350110/england-cricket-selector-luke-wright-on-how-players-are-picked-technique-character-and-facing-the-short-ball)). Australia's coach cited conditions and venue, recent form, workload rotation and depth ([cricket.com.au](https://www.cricket.com.au/news/4213686/australia-squad-world-test-championship-champions-trophy-odis-sri-lanka-caribbean-andrew-mcdonald-sam-konstas-cameron-green)). India backed out-of-form players with "a long rope" for continuity ([ESPNcricinfo, Jan 2025](https://www.espn.com/cricket/story/_/id/43626416/left-right-combination-reason-dhruv-jurel-batting-no-8-says-ryan-ten-doeschate)). No board publishes a weighted criteria list, and the research found no peer-reviewed study of selector bias. This split matters for the paper. The measurable criteria (form, role, conditions) can go into both the solver and the LLM prompt. The judgement criteria (character, continuity, reputation) can only enter through the LLM. There they are useful, but they are also how media stories and remembered reputations can sneak into the "reasons".

## Pitch reports are private, so use venue history as "conditions"

### How pitches are actually assessed

Pitch behaviour has a physical basis, but nobody publishes the measurements. An ECB research report found that umpires value **consistency of bounce** most. Hardness, measured with a Clegg hammer, predicts bounce. Good topsoils contain about 27–33% clay. **Live green grass matters more than total grass**: 40% cover with 50% green gives more bounce than 60% cover with 20% green ([ECB report](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/pitch-properties-and-performance-315/2437500)). The ball keeps about 93% of its speed on a fast pitch and about 85% on a slow one. Umpires reported moderate turn once more than about 15% of the spinners' landing area was damaged ([ECB report](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/pitch-properties-and-performance-315/2437500)). In public, pitch reading is visual and spoken. Before the December 2025 MCG Test, Steve Smith described "10 millimetres of grass, quite furry, quite green" and picked four quicks and no spinner ([Dawn](https://www.dawn.com/news/1963165)). Twenty wickets fell on day one, and the referee rated the pitch "Unsatisfactory" ([ICC media release](https://www.icc-cricket.com/media-releases/melbourne-pitch-rated-as-unsatisfactory)).

The ICC's official rating does not help a pre-match model. Since the 2024 revision the scale is Very Good, Satisfactory, Unsatisfactory or Unfit, with 1 and 3 demerit points for the bottom two ([ICC media release](https://www.icc-cricket.com/media-releases/melbourne-pitch-rated-as-unsatisfactory)). The referee gives the rating **after** the match, partly from what happened in it. Adverse ratings are rare: **6 of 135 Test pitches** were rated below average between the 2019 World Cup and the end of 2022 ([ESPNcricinfo](https://www.espncricinfo.com/story/is-the-icc-s-pitch-rating-system-fit-for-purpose-1365110)). As a feature it would leak the outcome and would barely vary, so leave it out.

### How pitch type maps to batter, pace or spin advantage

| Pitch type | What people see | Physical cause | Who it helps | Effect on selection | Proxy you can compute |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Green / seaming | Live grass, moisture | Grass and moisture give seam and bounce | Pace bowlers | Extra seamer, fewer spinners | High pace share of venue wickets; low first-innings average |
| Dry / turning | Bare, cracked, worn | Low moisture, damaged surface, more friction | Spinners | Extra spinner | High spin share of venue overs and wickets |
| Flat / true | Hard, even | Consistent bounce | Batters | Batting depth, a sixth bowling option | High venue first-innings average |
| Slow / low | Dull, two-paced | Low pace and bounce | Spinners and cutters; anchors | Spin, steady batters | Low first-innings average, low spinner economy |

The table summarises the ECB physics ([ECB report](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/pitch-properties-and-performance-315/2437500)) and the language referees use: "excessive seam movement, low and variable bounce" at Lord's in 2026, and "slow and low, with early help for spin" at Lahore ([IANS](https://ianslive.in/lords-gaddafi-stadium-handed-demerit-points-as-icc-rates-pitches-unsatisfactory--20260609181114)). Venue extremes are real in T20 too. At New York in the 2024 T20 World Cup the average first-innings score was **107**, and the pitch was rated Unsatisfactory ([NewsBytes](https://www.newsbytesapp.com/news/sports/t20-world-cup-icc-rates-new-york-tarouba-pitches-unsatisfactory/story)). Over a long era pace has been more effective than spin: in Test cricket from Dec 2017 to Apr 2020 pace averaged 26.26 and spin 34.14 ([ESPNcricinfo](https://www.espncricinfo.com/story/_/id/29269215/why-greatest-age-fast-bowling-60-years)). The balance still changes by country and period, which is why a venue-level number beats a global rule.

The key point for a T20I paper is that **pitch information changes one decision: the pace/spin split, usually by one slot**. A T20I lasts 40 overs, so the pitch has little time to wear. The famous "fourth-innings wear" story belongs to Tests, and even there recent data shows the third innings is now harder than the fourth ([Wisden](https://wisden.com/series-stories/england-v-india-2022/why-the-third-innings-not-the-fourth-innings-is-now-the-hardest-time-to-bat-in-test-cricket)). So build one abstract "venue conditions" block from earlier T20Is at the same venue: the spin share of overs bowled, and the mean first-innings score. Shrink both toward the host-country mean, because many venues have hosted only a few matches. The ablation is simple: run A with and without this block and test whether the LLM's number of spinners changes, and whether it changes in the right direction (more spinners where the venue spin share is high). That answers the TA's question with a measurement rather than an assumption.

### Weather has weak evidence; leave it out

Weather looks attractive but fails the TA's test. Sheffield Hallam and Auckland researchers found **no link between humidity and swing**. They suggested cloud cover might reduce air turbulence, but called this a hypothesis to test ([ICC news](https://www.icc-cricket.com/news/long-held-cricket-swing-theory-does-not-hold-water); [SHURA](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/5619)). Dew is often blamed for chasing wins in night T20s. Wisden argued that the IPL's two-ball dew rule "aims to solve a problem that doesn't exist" ([Wisden](https://www.wisden.com/series/ipl-2025/cricket-news/the-new-two-ball-rule-aims-to-solve-a-problem-that-doesnt-exist)), and the research found no study that measures a dew effect. In 589 decisive T20Is to 2017, sides batting first won 300 and chasing sides 289, close to a coin toss. The rate differed by host country, from 62.0% bat-first wins in Sri Lanka to 43.1% in South Africa ([ESPNcricinfo, 2017](https://www.espncricinfo.com/story/ananth-narayanan-analyses-t20-results-1092099)). In 1,228 ODIs, day/night status had no significant effect for any team ([SA J Sports Med](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10805135)). The practical problems are also heavy. Cricsheet has no start time or day/night flag, and the Venue Register has no coordinates, so you would need to geocode venues by hand. Open-Meteo's historical API is free and provides dew point and cloud cover ([Open-Meteo docs](https://open-meteo.com/en/docs/historical-weather-api)), but weather mostly affects the toss decision. The XI is fixed before the toss. Leave weather out and list it as future work.

## Fitness data is not public; the squad already handles it

Boards do use fitness gates, but they work as pass/fail eligibility, not as a graded input. In February 2021 India raised its Yo-Yo level to 17:1, with a 2 km time-trial option of 8:15 for fast bowlers and 8:30 for others. Some players failed and then passed a retest ([ESPNcricinfo, 2021](https://www.espn.co.uk/cricket/story/_/id/30886280/yo-yo-level-raised-171-trial-2k-run)). India added the Bronco shuttle test before the 2025 Asia Cup ([Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/sport/cricket/why-team-indias-fitness-coach-adrian-le-roux-introduced-the-bronco-test-ahead-of-asia-cup-2025-1.500266712)). The research found no numeric selection thresholds for England or Australia, and no player's test scores are ever published. A player who failed would simply not be in the squad. Your candidate pool already represents "available".

Workload management is real and mostly concerns fast bowlers. Hulin and colleagues found that when a fast bowler's 1-week load exceeded his 4-week average, injury risk the following week roughly doubled (RR 2.1). Above 200% it rose to RR 3.3 for balls bowled ([Hulin et al. 2014](https://unleash.catapult.com/wp-content/uploads/research//Cricket/Hulin%20(2014)%20Acute%20Workload%20Injury%20Risk%20Cricket.pdf)). Orchard and colleagues found that more than 50 match overs in 5 days raised injury risk in the next month (RR 1.54) ([Orchard et al. 2015](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_1660403751)). Fast bowlers were unavailable **20.6%** of the time against 12.5% for all players, and lumbar stress fractures caused 15% of all missed playing time ([Orchard, Kountouris & Sims 2016](https://www.dovepress.com/incidence-and-prevalence-of-elite-male-cricket-injuries-using-updated--peer-reviewed-article-OAJSM)). The acute:chronic ratio is also disputed. Including the acute week in the chronic window creates a correlation of 0.52 even between independent loads, against 0.01 without it ([Lolli et al.](https://core.ac.uk/download/322320894.pdf); [Impellizzeri et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7534938)). In practice, rest is planned before a series. India decided before the 2025 England tour that Bumrah would play only three of five Tests ([ICC](https://www.icc-cricket.com/news/gambhir-reveals-bumrah-plan-for-remaining-england-tests)). That is a squad-level availability decision, not a match-day score.

The research found **no public structured dataset of international injuries, fitness results or rest decisions**. Cricsheet records only in-match events such as retired hurt and injury or concussion substitutes ([Cricsheet JSON format](https://cricsheet.org/format/json/)). You could compute balls bowled in the last 7 or 28 days, but your data covers internationals only. Franchise and domestic loads would be missing, and "days since last match" mixes injury, rest and being dropped. That creates a leakage and endogeneity risk. So for the paper, state that availability is handled by the candidate pool and that workload-aware selection is open future work. The research found no cricket selection paper that includes workload, so this is a real gap you can point to.

## One overs-quota rule explains most format differences

The abstract concepts that carry across formats are bowling capacity, role coverage, conditions fit and form against reputation. One rule changes between formats: how many overs one bowler may bowl. Limited-overs cricket caps each bowler at one-fifth of the innings ([Wikipedia: Twenty20](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twenty20)). Tests have no cap, so the binding limit becomes taking 20 wickets.

| | T20I | ODI | Test |
|---|---|---|---|
| Overs per bowler | 4 of 20 | 10 of 50 | No cap |
| Minimum bowlers needed | 5 | 5 | None by rule; 4–5 frontline in practice |
| Fielding phases | Overs 1–6: 2 outside circle; 7–20: 5 | 1–10: 2; 11–40: 4; 41–50: 5 | No powerplays |
| Most valued skills | Powerplay and death scoring, economy | New-ball wickets, middle-overs control (spin) | Wicket-taking, then batting depth |
| Binding question | Enough bowling overs without losing hitters | Five bowlers plus batting depth at 6–7 | Can this attack take 20 wickets? |

The phase and cap rules come from [Wikipedia: Powerplay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Powerplay_(cricket)) and [Wikipedia: Twenty20](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twenty20). In ODIs the cost of a new-ball wicket fell to 31–32 runs in the two years before the 2023 World Cup, from 41 in 2019 ([ESPNcricinfo, 2023](https://www.espncricinfo.com/ci/content/story/1401076.html)). From July 2025, ODIs use two new balls, but only one from over 35 onward, to bring back reverse swing ([cricket.com](https://www.cricket.com/news/what-are-the-new-icc-playing-conditions-set-to-be-enforced-from-june-2025-5312025-1748664046218)). In Tests, "taking 16 wickets wins nothing. Bowling teams out twice does" ([cricketingview](https://cricketingview.substack.com/p/why-pakistan-arent-winning-and-why)). Picking a batting all-rounder can cost bowling: India's Shardul Thakur bowled only 16 of 182.4 overs at Headingley in 2025 ([ESPNcricinfo, 2025](https://www.espncricinfo.com/story/eng-vs-ind-2nd-test-time-for-india-to-show-how-badly-they-want-20-wickets-1493011)).

One T20 warning matters for your simulator and prompts. Do not judge T20I players by IPL norms. T20 World Cups produced 18 totals of 200+ in 317 matches, against 52 in one recent IPL season. In 2024, **36.5% of World Cup innings ended below 130** ([ESPNcricinfo, Feb 2026](https://www.espncricinfo.com/page2/content/story/1522550.html)). The IPL Impact Player rule pushed average IPL totals to 191 in 2024 ([ESPNcricinfo, Date 2024](https://www.espncricinfo.com/ci/content/story/1436641.html)). Your data is internationals only, so you avoid this problem. Say so in the paper.

For the paper, keep the experiments to **T20I only**. Your T20I simulator is validated (Brier 0.209 against 0.250, AUC 0.72), and your ODI simulator has no skill, so ODI results would have no outcome measure. Use the table above in one short "generalisation" paragraph. The verifier and repair MILP take the overs cap as a parameter (4/20 for T20I, 10/50 for ODI), so extending to ODI changes the parameter, not the method. Tests would need a different objective (wicket-taking capacity) and are clearly future work.

## Check the team in four layers: valid, legal, good, honest

### The rule set the verifier checks

Write the rules once, give each an ID, and use the same text in the LLM prompt and in the verifier code. Violations can then be counted per rule. The verifier is plain Python. It never trusts the LLM's own claims and always recomputes everything, as ConstraintBench does ([ConstraintBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.22465v2)).

| ID | Rule | Type | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| R0 | Output parses; 11 IDs, all distinct | Hard | Valid output |
| R1 | Every ID is in this match's candidate pool | Hard | Catches invented or ineligible players (entity hallucination) |
| R2 | At least 1 wicketkeeper | Hard | Practice; E3 constraint |
| R3 | At least 5 bowling options (4-over cap × 5 = 20 overs) | Hard | ICC cl. 13.9.1 |
| R4 | At least 1 spinner and at least 2 pace bowlers | Soft (report only) | Convention; uses `player_meta` |
| R5 | 6th bowling option present | Soft (report only) | Convention ([ESPNcricinfo](https://www.espncricinfo.com/page2/content/story/1522550.html)) |

Selector C is your E3 MILP with rules R1–R3, which it already has. Do not add the soft rules to C. Report them only as descriptions, so that C stays the existing, validated system.

### Fix the T20I objective before you compare anything

Your T20I run model does not beat career average. Do not use it as C's objective coefficient. Use a simple, transparent point-in-time player value instead. For batting, take runs scored above what a league-average batter would score from the same balls. For bowling, take runs saved compared with league-average economy over the balls bowled. Both are per match, from strictly earlier T20Is, and shrunk toward zero for players with few matches. This is the runs-above-average idea used by cricWAR ([Sloan SSAC](https://www.sloansportsconference.com/research-papers/cricwar-a-reproducible-system-for-evaluating-player-performance-in-limited-overs-cricket)). Including the bowling term matters. If the objective counts only batting runs, the solver will fill the five bowling slots with the bowlers who bat best, and the LLM will "lose" on the objective whenever it picks sensible specialist bowlers. Show the same value column to the LLM in the main condition, so A and C get identical information (ConstraintBench's fairness principle). Your validated simulator is the outcome measure, so the objective is only a proxy. If the simulator and the objective use the same player inputs, C gets a built-in advantage on simulated win probability. Disclose this.

### Selector B: minimal-change repair

B1 is one small MILP, and it is your own design: the research found no paper that publishes this exact repair. Let L be A's XI after removing invalid IDs. First maximise how many of L's players are kept, subject to R1–R3. Then, with that overlap fixed, maximise the player-value objective. In practice, use one objective, M × overlap + value, with M large.

```
max  M * sum_{i in L} x_i  +  sum_i v_i x_i
s.t. sum_i x_i = 11;  sum_{keepers} x_i >= 1;  sum_{bowling options} x_i >= 5;  x_i in {0,1}, i in pool
```

Swaps = 11 − overlap. The share of A's XIs that need 0 swaps equals A's feasibility rate. B2, sending the violation list back to the LLM for up to 3 rounds and then falling back to B1, is optional. "Formalize, Don't Optimize" found that refinement rounds mostly fix format and crash errors rather than improving solutions ([arXiv 2605.12421](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.12421)), so B2 is the first thing to cut if you run short of time.

### How the LLM "checks" its own team

The LLM's part in checking is to make claims that code can test. Ask for JSON like this:

```json
{"xi": ["P03", "..."], "keeper": "P07", "bowling_options": ["P02","P05","P09","P10","P11"],
 "reasons": [{"player_id": "P03", "claims": [{"type": "stat", "text": "...", "feature": "recent_sr", "op": ">", "value": 140}]}],
 "omissions": [{"player_id": "P14", "type": "quality|rule|conditions", "text": "..."}]}
```

The `keeper` and `bowling_options` fields are the LLM's self-check. Compare them with the verifier's result to get a **self-check accuracy**: how often the LLM says its XI is legal when it is not. This is the "fidelity" idea from "Formalize, Don't Optimize": the share of confident answers that are actually correct ([arXiv 2605.12421](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.12421)). Do not rely on the LLM to catch its own errors without outside feedback. That kind of self-critique has been shown to be unreliable ([Valmeekam et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.08118); [Huang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01798)).

### Evaluation criteria, in one table

| Layer | Metric | A | B | C |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Valid | Parse rate; invented-player rate (R0, R1) | Yes | Input check | — |
| Legal | Feasibility rate with Wilson 95% CI; violations per XI; per-rule counts | Yes | 100% by construction | 100% |
| Legal | Self-check accuracy (claimed keeper/bowlers vs verifier) | Yes | — | — |
| Good | Objective gap to C = (v(C) − v(X)) / v(C); % within ε = 0.1% and 1% | Feasible XIs only | Yes | 0 |
| Good | Swaps needed in repair | — | Yes | — |
| Good | **Simulated win probability vs the opponent's actual XI** (main outcome) | Feasible XIs only | Yes | Yes |
| Good | Overlap with the actual fielded XI (reference only, not ground truth) | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Honest | Claim precision = supported / (supported + contradicted); unverifiable share | Yes | Stale-reason share | — |
| Honest | "Why not X?" agreement with the MILP counterfactual | Yes | — | Provides the answer |
| Honest | Counterfactual consistency (change the cited feature, see whether the pick changes) | Subset | — | — |
| Robust | Named vs anonymised; spread across 3 samples | Yes | Yes | — |

The metric definitions follow ConstraintBench (feasibility, gap, joint % optimal) and COMPASS (acceptable vs near-optimal) ([ConstraintBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.22465v2); [COMPASS](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.07043v1)). Several XIs may score the same, so use the gap, not an exact match with C's XI. For the simulator, use common random numbers: give A, B and C the same seeds for each match, and run enough simulations that the simulator's own standard error is much smaller than the differences you report.

### Checking the reasons

Run four checks, cheapest first. **(1) Claim checking.** Each structured claim names a feature, an operator and a value. Code checks it against the exact table the LLM saw and labels it supported, contradicted or unverifiable. "Unverifiable" means the claim is not in the table, which suggests outside knowledge. This is FActScore's atomic-claim idea ([Min et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14251)) with code as the judge. Hand-label 50–100 claims to measure how accurate the automatic labels are. **(2) "Why not X?"** For each player the LLM leaves out with a reason, re-solve C with that player forced in. Record the objective cost and which rule becomes binding. A rule-based reason ("would leave only four bowlers") is faithful if forcing the player in makes R3 bind. A quality-based reason is faithful if the objective drops. This is contrastive explanation by model restriction ([Krarup et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.15575)), and people naturally ask "why P rather than Q" ([Miller](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.07269)). **(3) Counterfactual consistency.** On a subset, change the feature the LLM cited, for example halve the cited strike rate, re-ask, and check whether the pick changes in the predicted direction. This builds on counterfactual simulatability, which found that LLM explanations often score low on precision ([Chen et al., ICML 2024](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/chen24bl.html)). **(4) Stale reasons in B.** After repair, count how many of A's reasons refer to players B removed. That count is itself a finding about how reasons and decisions separate. Skip mechanistic methods such as NeuroFaith, which need model activations ([NeuroFaith](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.09277v4)), and cite them as related work.

### Memorisation, statistics and honest limits

The biggest threat is that the LLM remembers the real XI. When names were hidden in "Mystery Blocksworld", planning success fell from 82% to about 10% ([LLM-Modulo](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.01817v3)). Hidden names expose dependence on memory. Run three defences. First, choose held-out matches after the model's training cutoff, and check that date in the provider's documentation. Second, run an anonymised condition (P01–P18, shuffled order) next to the named one. Third, run a direct probe ("list India's XI against X on date D") and score its overlap with the real XI ([Golchin & Surdeanu](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.08493)). Prompt wording alone can move results by large amounts ([Sclar et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.11324)), so keep the prompt fixed, and add a paraphrase check only if time allows.

The match is the unit of analysis. If you run several samples per match, average them within the match first. B and C are feasible by construction, so do not test feasibility against them. Report A's feasibility with a Wilson CI. For per-match differences in win probability and gap, use the Wilcoxon signed-rank test and a paired bootstrap 95% CI (10,000 resamples of matches). Apply a Holm correction across 3–5 pre-chosen comparisons ([Dror et al.](https://aclanthology.org/P18-1128/)). Expect many ties where B equals C, and report how many. With about 100 matches only medium-to-large effects will be detectable, so call this an exploratory workshop study.

## Conclusion

The honest headline is that "more context" is the wrong lever for this paper. The context that real selectors use (pitch feel, dew, fitness tests, workload) is either not public, measured after the match, or weakly supported by evidence. Only venue history passes the test of being pre-match, measurable and linked to a real decision (spinner or seamer). This is good news for your scope. It frees the nine days for the part nobody has done: treating the LLM's XI and its reasons as claims that a sound symbolic checker can verify. The likely result is that A is often feasible but sometimes breaks the bowling-options or keeper rule, and that B recovers most of C's simulated win probability with few swaps. The more interesting finding will probably be in the reasons: how many are true, how many come from outside memory, and how many become stale after repair. That points to a design principle that holds beyond cricket, where the LLM explains, the solver guarantees, and code audits the explanation.

Two uncertainties should shape how you write the results. First, your objective is a proxy, and your T20I run model does not beat career average. So present simulated win probability as the main outcome and the objective gap as a diagnostic. Disclose any shared inputs between the simulator and the objective. Second, if the named and anonymised conditions differ a lot, memorisation is part of the story. Report that as a result, not a flaw: it tells readers when an LLM's cricket judgement is reasoning from the table and when it is recalling what it has read.
